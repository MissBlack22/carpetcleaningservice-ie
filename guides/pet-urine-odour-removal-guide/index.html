Pet Stains & Odour Guide

How to remove pet urine stains and smell from carpet

A pet accident on carpet is common, and how you respond in the first few minutes makes a real difference to whether the smell lingers. Here's an honest, DIY-first approach — what to do straight away, why some common instincts actually make odour worse, and when it's genuinely a job that needs more than a home remedy.

Act fast: blot, don't scrub

The moment you find a fresh accident, blot up as much liquid as possible with clean paper towel or an old towel, pressing down firmly and repeating with dry sections until no more moisture lifts out. Don't scrub or rub — that spreads the urine further into the carpet fibres and, more importantly, pushes it deeper toward the backing and underlay, which is exactly where you don't want it to go.

Why heat and steam can make things worse on a fresh stain

It's tempting to reach for a steam cleaner straight away, but heat is one of the worst things you can apply to a fresh urine stain. Heat can set the odour-causing compounds into the carpet fibres rather than lifting them out, making the smell more permanent rather than less. Cold water is the safer option for rinsing and diluting a fresh spot — save steam or hot water extraction cleaning for a professional visit once the area has been properly treated, not as a first response.

Why an enzyme-based cleaner works better than a general carpet cleaner

Regular carpet shampoo or general-purpose cleaners are built to lift dirt and freshen fabric, not to break down the specific compounds in pet urine that cause lingering odour. Enzyme-based (biological) cleaners work differently — they contain enzymes that break down the source of the smell at a molecular level, rather than covering it with fragrance. For a fresh, surface-level accident, applying an enzyme-based cleaner according to its instructions, letting it sit for the recommended time, and then blotting it out is usually more effective than any amount of scrubbing with a standard cleaner.

Why the smell can come back even after cleaning the surface

This is the part that catches most people out. Carpet isn't a single layer — underneath the visible pile there's a backing, then underlay or padding, and then the sub-floor beneath that. Urine, especially from repeated accidents in the same spot, can soak straight through the carpet into the underlay and even the floor beneath. Once it's down there, cleaning the surface of the carpet does nothing for the source of the smell — which is exactly why a spot can look and smell fine on a dry day and start smelling again when the room gets warm or humid.

A rough way to check how deep an accident has gone: press down firmly on the dried spot. If it still feels slightly damp, spongy, or the smell is noticeably stronger when you do this, there's a good chance moisture has reached the underlay.

Prevention while you're treating a spot

Keep pets away from the area while it's being treated and drying, both to give the treatment a chance to work and because pets are often drawn back to a spot they've used before, which can restart the cycle. If accidents are frequent or a new pattern for your pet, it's also worth mentioning to your vet — sudden or repeated house-training issues can sometimes have a physical cause worth ruling out, separate from the carpet cleaning itself.

Where DIY genuinely reaches its limit

To be upfront about it: this approach handles fresh, one-off, surface-level accidents well. It's honest to say that older stains, repeated accidents in the same spot, or any smell that returns after cleaning usually mean urine has reached the underlay or sub-floor — and no amount of surface cleaning or air freshener is going to fix that. At that point it needs proper extraction and treatment reaching below the carpet itself, which isn't something a home clean can achieve.
