Stain Removal Guide

Common carpet stains: how to remove them

Red wine, coffee, mud, chewing gum and grease are the marks we get asked about most. Here's honest, practical first-aid for each one — what actually helps, what makes things worse, and when a stain is better left to a professional.

The golden rule for any fresh stain: blot, don't rub. Press a clean, dry cloth or paper towel onto the mark and lift it away — rubbing just spreads the stain and pushes it deeper into the pile. Work from the outer edge of the stain inward so you're not enlarging it. Always test any cleaning solution on a hidden patch of carpet first.

Red wine Coffee & tea Mud Chewing gum Grease & oil

Red wine

Blot up as much of the spill as you can straight away, working inward from the edges. Once you've lifted the excess, apply a small amount of cold water to the area and continue blotting — cold water helps dilute the stain without setting it. Avoid hot water, which can fix the colour into the fibres. A mild washing-up liquid diluted in cold water can help lift what's left; apply sparingly with a cloth, blot, and rinse with a little more cold water to stop any residue attracting dirt afterwards.

If the wine has had time to dry in, don't panic — it's often still treatable, but it will usually need a more thorough approach than a quick blot, and repeated home attempts can sometimes make a dried stain harder to shift. That's a good point to get in touch rather than keep experimenting.

Coffee & tea

Coffee and tea are tannin-based stains, so the same rule applies: cold water, not hot. Blot the spill immediately, then work a small amount of cold water into the area with a clean cloth, blotting as you go. If milk or sugar was involved, treat it as you would a grease stain too (see below), since milk can leave a residue even after the visible coffee colour is gone.

Older tea and coffee stains that have dried can leave a faint tan mark that's genuinely difficult to fully remove without professional treatment, particularly on lighter carpets — we'd rather tell you that honestly than promise a home fix will get it perfect.

Mud

Mud is one of the few stains that's easier to deal with dry than wet. Let it dry out fully rather than trying to wipe it while damp — wiping wet mud tends to smear it further into the pile. Once dry, vacuum thoroughly, then use a stiff (but carpet-safe) brush to lift any remaining crumbs. If a faint mark is left afterwards, a little cold water and mild detergent, blotted rather than rubbed, usually finishes the job.

Given the amount of rain Dublin gets, mud and general footpath grime in hallways is one of the most common things we're asked to treat — often it's less a "stain" than general soiling that's built up over time, which a full clean handles better than spot treatment.

Chewing gum

Never try to pull gum straight out of carpet — it stretches the fibres and spreads the gum further. Instead, harden it first: fill a sandwich bag with ice cubes and hold it against the gum for several minutes until it's brittle. Once hard, gently scrape it away with a blunt edge, like the back of a spoon or a dull knife, working from the edges of the gum inward. Any small residue left behind can usually be lifted with a little rubbing alcohol on a cloth, dabbed rather than rubbed, then rinsed with cold water.

Grease & oil

Grease and oil-based marks behave differently from drink or food stains because they're not water-soluble, so water alone won't shift them. Start by blotting up any excess, then cover the mark generously with a dry absorbent powder — baking soda or cornflour both work — and leave it to sit for at least 15–20 minutes so it can draw the oil out of the fibres. Vacuum the powder away, then treat any remaining mark with a small amount of washing-up liquid in cold water, blotted in and rinsed out.

Cooking oil, butter and similar kitchen spills are the most common grease stains we see, and they're also one of the marks most likely to reappear faintly after a home attempt if the oil wasn't fully drawn out — worth keeping an eye on over the following day or two.

When a stain is a job for a professional

General household advice like this handles a lot of everyday spills well, especially when they're treated quickly. But some stains have set in too deep, reacted with the wrong cleaning product, or been treated with heat that's locked them in — and no amount of extra scrubbing at home will bring those back. If a stain hasn't responded after one careful attempt, stop rather than keep applying product to it; further attempts can sometimes make professional treatment harder, not easier.

Frequently asked questions

Should I rub a stain to get it out?

No. Rubbing pushes the stain deeper into the carpet fibres and can damage the pile. Always blot gently from the outside of the mark inward, using a clean, dry cloth or paper towel.

Does hot water help remove carpet stains?

Not usually. Heat can permanently set many stains, especially protein and tannin-based ones like wine, tea and coffee. Cold or lukewarm water is the safer choice for most fresh spills.

Can I use my own steam cleaner on a fresh stain?

Be cautious. Steam applies heat, which can set certain stains rather than lift them. It's usually safer to blot and treat a fresh stain first, and leave steam or hot water extraction cleaning to a professional visit if the mark doesn't shift.
